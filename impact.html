Impact

Part of a global movement

Roughly two-thirds of the world has no access to MRI at all. Over the past few years, a growing network of universities, hospitals, and independent teams has been proving that a working MRI scanner can be designed in the open, built with ordinary tools, and shared freely — for a fraction of the cost of a conventional machine. Community MRI Project's own build draws directly on that movement, especially the OSI² ONE open-source scanner.

Built around the world

Leiden & Delft, Netherlands

Where OSI² ONE began

The Open Source Imaging Initiative first presented OSI² ONE in 2023 — a modular, 50 mT scanner built from open hardware (released under the CERN Open Hardware Licence) and open software (GPL-3.0), designed to be built with standard tools like CNC machines and 3D printers.

Source: Let's Data Science

Mbarara, Uganda & Asunción, Paraguay

Built locally, from local parts

Teams at Mbarara University of Science and Technology and in Paraguay each built their own OSI² ONE systems with local staff — Uganda's build took about 11 days, Paraguay's took roughly 6 months sourcing most components locally. Both scanners are now used in neuroimaging research, including studies on Alzheimer's disease.

Source: Alzheimer's & Dementia (PMC)

Berlin, Germany & 19 partners across Europe

A European reference system

Germany's national metrology institute (PTB) leads A4IM, an EU-funded project building on OSI² ONE to create a fully documented, affordable open-source low-field MRI reference system — aiming to make repairs cheaper and open a path toward regulatory approval for under-resourced hospitals.

Source: PTB / OpenProject

Cape Town, South Africa & Montreal, Canada

Proving the design travels

Led by McGill's Udunna Anazodo with partners at Stellenbosch University, NYU Abu Dhabi, and the Polytechnic University of Valencia, the Imaging Without Borders project built two prototype scanners in parallel — one in Cape Town, one in Montreal — showing that an open-source design can be reliably reproduced by different teams in different places.

Source: The Neuro, McGill University
